If you’ve ever seen “cooperative” or “cave coopérative” on a wine label, you’ve already encountered one of the most important business models in wine. A clone is not a different grape variety. It’s the same grape, copied over and over from cuttings, with tiny natural changes that build up over time. And those little changes matter. Some clones give smaller berries and more concentration. Some ripen earlier. Some bring more perfume, more spice, or a more herbal style. That’s why two bottles made from the same grape can still taste different. It’s one more piece of the puzzle — and honestly, a pretty fun one once you know what to listen for.

Over time, tiny natural mutations show up. And growers notice that some vines do different things — maybe smaller berries, earlier ripening, more perfume, more structure. So when a winemaker talks about clone 115 or clone 667, they’re talking about one of the tools they use to shape the wine before it even gets into the cellar. That’s why two wines can be made from the same grape and still taste different. Same grape. Different clone. Different vibe.

LinkedIn

Wine clone conversations can sound technical fast, but they’re really about one of the most practical decisions in the vineyard: how a grower and winemaker choose to shape style. A clone is not a different grape variety. It’s a genetic line of the same grape, propagated from cuttings, with small natural mutations that can affect things like berry size, ripening, colour, tannin, aromatics, and yield. That’s why clonal selection matters. In Pinot Noir, for example, many producers plant several clones in the same block to build complexity. In Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, and Sauvignon Blanc, clone choice can also help steer the final style in a very deliberate way. For producers, this is one more layer of vineyard intent. For drinkers, it’s a useful reminder that the same grape name on a label does not always mean the same sensory result in the glass. It’s a small detail, but one that explains a lot.

Ramato is a great example of how winemaking method shapes both style and perception. If you’ve ever poured a Pinot Grigio and found a copper or pale pink hue instead of the pale straw colour most people expect, you’ve likely encountered ramato. The colour comes from extended skin contact, which also brings more texture and a little tannic grip. What’s especially interesting is that this is not a novelty. Ramato is the traditional style of Pinot Grigio from Friuli-Venezia Giulia in northeast Italy, predating the modern stainless steel, quick-press approach that became standard in the mid-20th century. For trade, it’s a useful reminder that consumer expectations around “white wine” are often shaped more by technology and market style than by grape identity alone. For anyone educating drinkers, ramato is a very approachable entry point into skin contact wines: familiar grape, surprising result, and a clear story behind the glass.
