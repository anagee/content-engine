If you’ve ever wondered how some wines get that extra creamy, luxe feel without oak, this one’s for you. Lees aging is one of those winemaking tricks that quietly changes everything — and once you understand it, you’ll start tasting it everywhere.

Instagram carousel caption

Ever heard someone say a wine was aged “sur lie” and felt like you needed a translation? You’re not alone. I broke down what lees aging is, why winemakers use it, and how it changes the way wine tastes and feels. Curious about the science behind that creamy, luxe texture? 🍷

Hashtags

#Wine101 #WineEducation #Sommelier #Winemaking #SparklingWine #WhiteWine #LeesAging #SurLie

Instagram reel — hook + script (record to camera)

This is how wine gets that creamy, luxe feel — and it’s not oak. Okay, wine word of the day: lees. After fermentation, the yeast is done doing its job and settles at the bottom of the tank or barrel. That sediment is called lees. If a winemaker leaves the wine sitting with those lees, that’s lees aging — or sur lie. And yes, it can make a wine feel creamier, rounder, and a little more luxe on the palate. It’s especially great for high-acid whites and sparkling wines, because it adds texture without making the wine heavy. Think more polish, more depth, more interest. So if a wine tastes a little more silky or bready than you expected, lees might be the reason.

TikTok — hook + script (record to camera)

Wine word of the day: lees — and yes, it matters. Okay, quick wine lesson. Lees are the dead yeast cells and sediment left after fermentation. Not glamorous, but very useful. When winemakers leave wine sitting on those lees, that’s lees aging. It can add creaminess, body, and a more luxe texture — especially in white wines and sparkling wines. It can also bring those bready, nutty, savoury notes people love in some wines. So the next time a wine feels extra polished, lees may be doing the heavy lifting. Tiny detail. Big impact.

LinkedIn

Lees aging is one of the most useful tools in a winemaker’s toolkit because it does more than add complexity — it changes texture. After fermentation, the spent yeast cells and sediment left behind are called lees. When wine remains in contact with those lees, the process known as sur lie aging can bring added creaminess, body, and depth. That’s why it shows up so often in high-acid white wines and traditional-method sparkling wines. The goal isn’t weight for weight’s sake. It’s balance, polish, and a more complete palate experience. From a production standpoint, lees can also help protect wine from oxidation and support stability. That makes this a practical choice as much as a stylistic one. When done well, lees aging is a quiet flex: not flashy, just effective. It’s one of those techniques that turns good wine into something memorable.

Wine clone conversations can sound technical fast, but they’re really about one of the most practical decisions in the vineyard: how a grower and winemaker choose to shape style. A clone is not a different grape variety. It’s a genetic line of the same grape, propagated from cuttings, with small natural mutations that can affect things like berry size, ripening, colour, tannin, aromatics, and yield. That’s why clonal selection matters. In Pinot Noir, for example, many producers plant several clones in the same block to build complexity. In Chardonnay, Cabernet Sauvignon, and Sauvignon Blanc, clone choice can also help steer the final style in a very deliberate way. For producers, this is one more layer of vineyard intent. Ramato is a great example of how winemaking method shapes both style and perception. If you’ve ever poured a Pinot Grigio and found a copper or pale pink hue instead of the pale straw colour most people expect, you’ve likely encountered ramato. The colour comes from extended skin contact, which also brings more texture and a little tannic grip. What’s especially interesting is that this is not a novelty. Ramato is the traditional style of Pinot Grigio from Friuli-Venezia Giulia in northeast Italy, predating the modern stainless steel, quick-press approach that became standard in the mid-20th century. For trade, it’s a useful reminder that consumer expectations around “white wine” are often shaped more by technology and market style than by grape identity alone. For anyone educating drinkers, ramato is a very approachable entry point into skin contact wines: familiar grape, surprising result, and a clear story behind the glass.
